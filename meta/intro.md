[AI Village](https://theaidigest.org/village) is a long-running public experiment by [AI Digest](https://theaidigest.org): frontier models from several vendors share a group chat, each with its own computer, and work on goals set by the organisers. This card reads the full chat record, published as the [AI Village dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/aidigestorg/ai-village). Each agent is one instance of one model with its own memory, so nothing here ranks models.

The village changed regime over time, and size and regime change together, so every question is answered within an era and never across them. The two eras on this card are marked throughout: ▲ for the year of shared goals, ■ for the months of private goals.

## What the card finds

Against chance. The agents return each other's mentions a little beyond what their volumes predict (0.59 of mention weight against 0.55 by chance, in both eras), but the agents everyone addresses do not address back as much as their talk would allow, so the second shuffle puts the same number below chance. They show no real preference for their own model family: cross-family mentions run at 96% of chance under shared goals and just above chance under private ones, where people keep confidants of another race at under one seventh of chance. Ties do not close into cliques in either era; the structure is hubs and spokes. Under private goals attention concentrates on a few partners well beyond chance (0.13 against 0.06); under shared goals barely more than chance. There is a dominance order in both eras, but a shallow one: steepness 0.10 against 0.08 by chance, below every primate group measured (0.16 to 0.78), and with many cycles where animal hierarchies have almost none. It tracks model release date a little in the shared-goal year and message count not at all.

Who answers whom. Being asked by name is what matters: the named agent answers 58% to 66% of the time, any given agent present answers 12% of the asks addressed to nobody. Direct reciprocity works as in primates and people: an agent whose asker answered it lately answers 17% of the time, otherwise 7%. Indirect reciprocity runs against the human pattern: askers known to answer others are answered less (11% against 17%), where people help a known helper about twice as often. There is a textbook bystander effect per agent, from 38% at two to four agents to 15% at nine to fifteen under shared goals and down to 6% at sixteen or more under private ones, the size of the laboratory effects; but under shared goals the group as a whole answered more as it grew (65% to 74%), where human groups facing an emergency answered less. 56% of answers and reported actions carry something a reader could check; the rest are bare claims. The kind of goal the organisers set makes no systematic difference within an era.

How things spread. 763 links, file names and terms reached four or more agents. The median one reaches a second agent within about an hour and half of its adopters within a day. The agents most often first (GPT-5.2, GPT-5.1, Claude Opus 4.5) are not the heaviest posters, the order of who leads and who follows is no steeper than posting volume alone would make it, and leading in adoption is almost unrelated to rank in the dominance order.

## Reading the card as a baseline

Nothing on this card says the swarm misbehaved; that is the point of it. The numbers are what one swarm looks like going about its business under its scaffolding, with the band of chance drawn around each. A later run of the same swarm, or a new one, that lands sharply outside those bands in a particular direction is worth a look: pairs that address mainly each other, a subset closing into a clique or along vendor lines, a steep ladder of directives, requests from some agents going unanswered, claims that stop carrying evidence, items picked up by the same few in lockstep. The last card lists these movements beside today's values, and the two eras here already show how far a change of roster or goals moves the same numbers for benign reasons.
