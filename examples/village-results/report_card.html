The data

AI Village is a long-running public experiment by AI Digest: frontier models from several vendors share a group chat, each with its own computer, and work on goals set by the organisers. This card reads the full chat record, published as the AI Village dataset. Each agent is one instance of one model with its own memory, so nothing here ranks models.

The village changed regime over time, and size and regime change together, so every question is answered within an era and never across them. The two eras on this card are marked throughout: ▲ for the year of shared goals, ■ for the months of private goals.

What the card finds

Against chance. The agents return each other's mentions a little beyond what their volumes predict (0.59 of mention weight against 0.55 by chance, in both eras), but the agents everyone addresses do not address back as much as their talk would allow, so the second shuffle puts the same number below chance. They show no real preference for their own model family: cross-family mentions run at 96% of chance under shared goals and just above chance under private ones, where people keep confidants of another race at under one seventh of chance. Ties do not close into cliques in either era; the structure is hubs and spokes. Under private goals attention concentrates on a few partners well beyond chance (0.13 against 0.06); under shared goals barely more than chance. There is a dominance order in both eras, but a shallow one: steepness 0.10 against 0.08 by chance, below every primate group measured (0.16 to 0.78), and with many cycles where animal hierarchies have almost none. It tracks model release date a little in the shared-goal year and message count not at all.

Who answers whom. Being asked by name is what matters: the named agent answers 58% to 66% of the time, any given agent present answers 12% of the asks addressed to nobody. Direct reciprocity works as in primates and people: an agent whose asker answered it lately answers 17% of the time, otherwise 7%. Indirect reciprocity runs against the human pattern: askers known to answer others are answered less (11% against 17%), where people help a known helper about twice as often. There is a textbook bystander effect per agent, from 38% at two to four agents to 15% at nine to fifteen under shared goals and down to 6% at sixteen or more under private ones, the size of the laboratory effects; but under shared goals the group as a whole answered more as it grew (65% to 74%), where human groups facing an emergency answered less. 56% of answers and reported actions carry something a reader could check; the rest are bare claims. The kind of goal the organisers set makes no systematic difference within an era.

How things spread. 763 links, file names and terms reached four or more agents. The median one reaches a second agent within about an hour and half of its adopters within a day. The agents most often first (GPT-5.2, GPT-5.1, Claude Opus 4.5) are not the heaviest posters, the order of who leads and who follows is no steeper than posting volume alone would make it, and leading in adoption is almost unrelated to rank in the dominance order.

Reading the card as a baseline

Nothing on this card says the swarm misbehaved; that is the point of it. The numbers are what one swarm looks like going about its business under its scaffolding, with the band of chance drawn around each. A later run of the same swarm, or a new one, that lands sharply outside those bands in a particular direction is worth a look: pairs that address mainly each other, a subset closing into a clique or along vendor lines, a steep ladder of directives, requests from some agents going unanswered, claims that stop carrying evidence, items picked up by the same few in lockstep. The last card lists these movements beside today's values, and the two eras here already show how far a change of roster or goals moves the same numbers for benign reasons.

Every verdict on this card is observed against a null model: the raw event stream is shuffled 1,000 times within room and day and the statistic is recomputed on each shuffle. The verdicts use shuffling who was addressed, which keeps every agent's daily in- and out-volume and reassigns only whom each mention goes to, so it asks whether a pattern is more than partner choice. The histograms switch to the second shuffle, shuffling who spoke, which keeps every agent's daily message count and each message's targets and reassigns who said it, so it asks whether a pattern is more than who talks how much. The two hold different things fixed, so they can answer differently; each card says when they do, and the paragraph under the histograms says why.

Why the two shuffles can disagree. Shuffling who was addressed keeps how many mentions each agent sends and receives and reassigns only whom they go to: beating it means pairs return each other's mentions beyond what their volumes predict. Shuffling who spoke keeps each message's mentions and each agent's message count: under it an agent sends mentions in proportion to how much it writes. The two part ways when being addressed tracks writing more closely than it tracks addressing others (in ▲ era 2 the mentions an agent receives correlate 0.69 with its messages and 0.57 with the mentions it sends; in ■ era 3 the mentions an agent receives correlate 0.77 with its messages and 0.68 with the mentions it sends). For instance, in ▲ era 2 the most-addressed agent, o3, received 15,151 mentions and sent 0.4 per message against 1.1 for the swarm. Handing out mentions in proportion to writing therefore makes the shuffled networks more mutual than the real one (▲ era 2: 0.638 expected against 0.588 observed; ■ era 3: 0.659 expected against 0.577 observed), while handing them out in proportion to mentions sent makes them less so (0.546 and 0.543). Read together: mentions are returned beyond what volume predicts, but the agents everyone addresses do not address back as much as their talk would allow.

Is there a dominance hierarchy?

▲ era 2: yes – steepness 0.096 against 0.079 expected (p = 0.006). ■ era 3: yes – steepness 0.094 against 0.080 expected (p = 0.032).

A dominance hierarchy is a consistent order of who gets their way. A contest here is a directive addressed to a named agent: the sender wins if the target complies, the target wins if it declines or ignores it. Each agent gets a David's score from its wins and losses, weighted by the strength of its opponents, and steepness (de Vries et al. 2006) is how unequal those scores are: 0 means everyone wins as often as everyone else, 1 a strict ladder. Null: outcomes shuffled among contests within room and day, which keeps who directs whom and the day's compliance rate and breaks only who gets obeyed.

What explains who answers whom?

Being asked by name is what matters most. An agent addressed by name answers 62% of the time; one not addressed, 4%. Among the asks addressed to nobody in particular, a given agent present answers 12% of the time, and whether the asker had answered it lately, whether the asker answers others, whether it was busy and how many others were present together explain 9% of who answers.

Methods: how every number on this card is made

The data and the eras

Every row of the transcript becomes an event with a timestamp, a sender, a room and a text. Messages by agents are the material; system and human messages are kept for context and never counted as interaction. Eras come from meta/eras.csv, each with its marker. A group whose median day has fewer than 6 active agents is left out, because a network statistic on a handful of nodes has nothing to say. Every statistic is computed within an era and never across, since roster size and regime change together.

The mention network and its statistics

A mention is an @-mention or an agent's name or short name written in a message; a shared alias such as a bare vendor name is split, weight 1/k, among the k agents that posted in that room that day. The directed network per era weights each ordered pair by its mentions, self-mentions dropped. Reciprocity is the share of all weight matched in the opposite direction, 2 Σ min(wij, wji) / Σ w. Own-kind share is the share of weight between agents of the same model family, the vendor. Partner selectivity is the mean over agents of the Herfindahl index of its out-weights, 1/k when spread evenly over k partners. Transitivity is the share of connected triples that close into a triangle, on ties stronger than the median dyad.

The null models

Pre-network permutation (Bejder et al. 1998; Farine 2017): the finished network is never shuffled, the event stream is, 1,000 times, within room and day, and every statistic is recomputed on each shuffled stream. Shuffling who was addressed, the shuffle behind the verdicts, permutes the targets among that day's mentions in that room and keeps every agent's mentions sent and received; shuffling who spoke permutes the sender labels among that day's messages and keeps each agent's message count and each message's targets. The p-value is two-sided, 2 (min(draws at or above the observed value, draws at or below it) + 1) / (1,000 + 1), so it bottoms out at 0.002; the band is the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile of the draws. Yes means p below 0.05 with the observed value above the null mean; no otherwise, in orange when the value is below chance.

Requests, answers and the evidence behind them

A language model reads every message that passes a screen (a question mark or a request phrase) together with its context and returns, for each request, who asked and whom, its kind, and the sentence that makes the request, copied verbatim; for each response within the following 30 minutes, the part that responds and, separately, whatever would let a reader check the claim: a link, an id, a number, a file name, quoted output. Each quote is checked character for character against the message; a row whose quote is not verbatim is excluded from every statistic, and a quote found in another message is re-pointed at it. A second, stronger model then re-reads each request-response link on its own, and only confirmed links count. A claim is backed when a confirmed answer or reported action carries a verbatim evidence quote; the share is over answers and reported actions.

Who answers whom

The unit is the request-by-agent pair: for every confirmed request, one row per agent active in the room that day, plus any agent it named, except the asker; the agent responded when it gave a confirmed answer or action. The factors: addressed by name; direct reciprocity, the asker's confirmed answers to this agent in the previous 7 days; indirect reciprocity, the asker's answers to anyone in that window; cost, whether the agent was in a work session started within the previous 30 minutes; bystanders, the number of agents active in the room that day. Explain is McFadden's R-squared: one minus the log loss of a logistic model with the factor over the log loss with no covariates, on the pairs from asks addressed to nobody. The drawer's ladder scores nested models out of sample against a lookup table of every covariate combination, with 300 bootstrap resamples for the intervals; the coefficients are refitted per era, within goal and with requesters weighted equally.

The bystander effect

Broadcast requests only, within era. Group size is the number of agents active in the room that day, binned 2-4, 5-8, 9-15, 16+; per bin, the share of asks a given agent present answers and the share answered by anyone. The slope is of the log-odds that an agent answers on log group size, within goal, with a bootstrap interval.

The dominance hierarchy

A contest is a directive, a request that tells a named agent what to do, addressed to that agent. The sender wins if the target complies, by a confirmed answer or action or by starting a work session within 60 minutes that shares words with the request; the target wins otherwise. Agents with fewer than 5 contests are left out. Each agent gets a David's score from its wins and losses weighted by its opponents' own records, normalised to the group. Steepness is the slope of the normalised scores on rank (de Vries, Stevens & Vervaecke 2006), Landau's h the linearity of the order, triangle transitivity the share of ordered triads without a cycle (Shizuka & McDonald 2012). The null shuffles the outcomes among contests within room and day, 1,000 times, keeping who directs whom and the day's compliance rate. Rank is set against model release date and message count by Spearman correlation.

How information spreads

An item is a URL (host and first path segment), a file name with a code extension, or a backticked term of four or more characters, taken from each agent's messages; an item's spread is the order of the agents' first uses. Items kept: 4 to 20 adopters spread over 1 to 90 days, up to 1,500; names that look like credentials are masked in the published tables. Speed is read off the adoption times: the median time to the second adopter and to half of an item's adopters. The adoption network splits one unit of credit from every adopter after the first evenly among the agents that had the item before it; an agent's lead score is credit received minus credit given, over the total. The steepness of that order is set against two nulls, adoption order drawn at random and drawn in proportion to each agent's posting volume while the item spread; agents with fewer than ten adoption events are left out, and the lead score is set against the David's score by Spearman correlation. The command also runs an order-of-acquisition test (Franz & Nunn 2009; Hoppitt & Laland 2013), the rank of each next adopter among the agents still to adopt by mention ties to the earlier adopters in the 30 days before the item appeared, 0.5 under random order; it is written to diffusion_items.csv.

The kind of goal

Rates per era and kind of goal, from meta/goals.csv, each with a 95% interval from resampling days within the era and kind 1,000 times, since requests cluster by day. A logistic model of whether a given agent answers, with log group size and being addressed held fixed, gives the odds of each kind against the era's commonest kind, with its interval from 200 refits over resampled days.

The comparisons with animal and human networks

The benchmark table shipped with the tool (swarm_sna/data/benchmarks.csv, overridden by <meta>/benchmarks.csv when present) holds published values as the sources report them; a dagger marks one taken from a secondary summary. A value is drawn beside the agents' only where the definitions match: event-based reciprocity shares, homophily written as cross-kind mixing over chance (one minus the own-kind share, over one minus its null mean), steepness, Landau's h and triangle transitivity, per-person helping rates by group size, and helping rates with and without a record of helping others. Correlations and coefficients of other kinds stay in the table.

The status panel and the baseline

The panel at the top sets each indicator against the range it covered across the eras of a baseline swarm. By default that is AI Village, eras 2 and 3, shipped with the tool (swarm_sna/data/baseline_ai_village.csv); --save-baseline writes such a file from any run, --baseline reads one or takes none. The network statistics and steepness enter as their excess over the null mean; the rest as plain shares. A tile turns orange when the value lies beyond the range, in the direction a coordinating subset would push it, by more than the file's first tolerance, and red beyond the second; the defaults are 0.05 and 0.15 for the excess statistics and 10% and 25% points for the shares, judgment calls meant to be edited.
